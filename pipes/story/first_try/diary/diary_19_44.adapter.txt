The Car That Would Not Start

Tommy sat in the driver’s seat, fingers cold and numb on the steering wheel, the engine silent as a tomb. The key turned in the ignition—once, twice—and the car did not respond. No sputter, no cough, no whisper of life. Just the stillness of a room that had forgotten how to breathe. The headlights, which had flickered on when the world went dark, now lay dead, leaving the world outside a gray, unlit field. He could smell the metal of the engine block, hot and sour, like old pennies left in a drawer too long. The air was still, thick with the scent of gasoline that had dried into dust. He checked the dashboard—no lights, no beeping, no sign of movement. He tried again, then again, hands trembling slightly, as if the car might respond to a nervous touch. But it only remained, a silent, helpless thing, its breath stolen from the world. The cold wind through the cracked window carried with it a whine like a forgotten train, and for a moment, Tommy thought of the way the old girlfriend used to laugh when the wind came up—like it had a secret language. But he didn’t laugh. He just sat, the silence pressing into his ribs, and wondered if help was five miles away or five hundred, if the road would end in a town or in a field of weeds. No one came. No headlights. No siren. Just the empty road stretching ahead, and the weight of the moment that had already passed him by.

Southwick appeared at the edge of the ditch, his hat pulled low, a wool coat flapping like a banner in the wind. He stood there, one hand in his pocket, the other on his hip, watching the car as if it were a piece of art that had gone wrong. Tommy saw him first through the windshield, a silhouette against the sky, and his heart sank—not from fear, but from the sudden realization that Southwick had seen him. He had seen the car, the engine, the way it sat like a broken promise. But Tommy didn’t move. He didn’t speak. He didn’t even look up. He just stared at the road, the way the snow had settled into the cracks like ash. Southwick stood still, his eyes fixed on the car, as if it were a map of a forgotten town. Tommy felt the shame of the moment—of being stuck, of being unable to say the words that had lived in his throat for years: *“I need help. I can’t get my car started. I need to talk to you. I need to talk to the person who used to understand me.”* But the words were like gravel in his mouth, and he swallowed them down. Southwick didn’t speak either. He just looked at the car, then at Tommy, then at the sky, as if the sky might tell him something. Tommy could smell the old man’s wool—damp, earthy, like a forest floor after rain. The air tasted of pine and cold. He thought of the time they used to talk under the old oak in the park, when the world was still a place you could walk into and come out changed. Now the world was just a car, a road, and a man who couldn’t speak. Southwick didn’t approach, didn’t offer a word, just stood there, like a statue of patience, and Tommy knew—this was the moment he had to choose. To speak, or to walk.

Tommy thought of the old girlfriend, the one who once said, “You’re like a car with a flat tire—only you don’t know it’s flat until you try to move.” He thought of her voice, soft and knowing, and how she’d smile when he said things like “I can’t get the car started.” She’d say, “Oh, that’s the most honest thing I’ve heard in years.” And now, the car wouldn’t start. And Southwick stood there. And Tommy knew—this was the moment to ask. To say, “Southwick, I can’t start the car. I need help. I need to speak to the person who used to understand me.” But the shame came like a wave—like the cold that had settled into his bones. What if she’d been right? What if he had just been too afraid to admit it? What if the world had gone on without him realizing he was broken? The I Ching hexagram pulsed in the back of his mind—its pattern of silence and movement, like the way the wind moved through the trees. He thought of the way the old girlfriend used to say things were always just one word away from being understood. And then, the moment passed. It was like the sun rising too slowly, too quietly. The world kept turning, and Tommy realized he couldn’t stop it. He turned the key one last time. It didn’t work. He let his hands fall to his lap. Then, slowly, he reached for the door handle, pushed it open, and stepped out into the cold. He didn’t look back. He didn’t say anything to Southwick. He just walked, his boots crunching on frost, the wind biting at his neck. The world didn’t stop. The world didn’t care. But Tommy did.

Tommy walked home, his boots crunching on frost, the wind biting at his neck, and the world outside quiet and unkind. He passed a few houses, some with lights on, others dark and silent, like the way the old girlfriend had once said, “Some people just don’t know when to stop.” He thought of the way the roads were laid out, the way the trees leaned into the wind, and how the cold had made everything sharper, more real. He thought of the way the world had been so quiet, so content, so dry and clean. It reminded him of the time he’d sat in the James John Cafe, the air thick with the smell of coffee and old paper, and the way Southwick had said, “No, there is no wait. Only enjoy. Enjoy or Enjoy NOT.” He didn’t know what that meant. But it was the kind of thing that stayed with you. The world was cold, yes. But it was clean. Clean water for all. He thought of the way the Pogo Possum’s creator had immortalized the name “Anna,” and how the world had forgotten her. He thought of the way the old girlfriend had said, “We were so complacent that we were able to live normal lives.” And now, here he was, walking alone, the car behind him like a ghost, the moment to ask passed, and the world went on. He reached the edge of the road, where the car had broken down, and stopped. He looked back. The car sat there, silent, like a memory. He breathed deep, and walked on, his boots crunching into the frost, the wind whispering through the trees, and the I Ching hexagram still pulsing in the quiet.

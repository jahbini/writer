Hi, Friend,

You know that old Tom’s got a habit of letting his cars go to pieces like a slow guttering fire — the kind that doesn’t burn bright, just whispers and rusts in the corners. Well, this one happened on a dusk road, two lanes, wet asphalt, fog curling off like smoke from a dead engine. Tommy was in it, just Tommy, not even asking if the radio was working, just staring at the cracked windshield like it was a mirror to something he’d rather not see. The car gave one final sputter, then died — like a breath held too long, and the silence after? Like the world had just stopped breathing. He didn’t move, didn’t touch anything, just stood there, fingers pressed into the cold leather, watching the gap between the tires and the road, where the world had gone numb. The car wasn’t a machine anymore, it was a wall. And the wall said: “You’re here. You’re stuck.”

Then, a few minutes later, the fog thinned into a pale gray, and a red blink cut through the haze — a pickup, a faded blue coat, Southwick. He stopped halfway, leaned out, and just like that, opened the passenger door and stepped out, handing Tommy a folded piece of paper with a phone number in pencil. “Just a quick call,” he said, calm as a breeze, “Tommy. I know you don’t like asking for help. You don’t have to do it alone.” And he didn’t wait. Just handed it over, like it was a prayer in a paper boat. The paper trembled in the wind, like it knew what it meant to be given. Tommy stood frozen, hand still on the door handle, the paper in Southwick’s hand — trembling — like it was the first time anyone had ever offered to walk into the dark with him.

Late night, no fog, just stars through thin clouds. Old girlfriend’s voice crackled over a forgotten phone — “I just saw you sitting there, Tommy. You didn’t even look up when I passed. You’re not alone anymore.” Tommy turned, flinched toward the glove compartment like he’d grab a key, but instead slammed the door shut and stepped back. His boots crunching on gravel, he stared at the car, then at the empty road, and whispered, “I don’t need anyone to see me like this,” before turning his face to the wind and walking backward, like he was trying to escape the memory of the offer itself. His hands were clenched at his sides, like they were holding the shape of a door that wouldn’t open.

Now, dawn. Frost crawling up the fenders like veins of ice. A gust stirs it, and the dead battery hums faintly — a ghost of life. Tommy steps out, one foot, then the other, walks without looking back. Boots crunching on frost, hands loose, like they’ve forgotten how to grip. No words. No destination. Just the ground beneath his feet, and the need to move away from the silence. He didn’t speak to anyone, didn’t check the sky — just the earth, and the earth felt like a promise he’d finally given up on keeping.

And Southwick? He was there, in the middle of it, with the phone number, and the calm, and the way he didn’t even blink when Tommy didn’t take it. He just said, “You don’t have to do it alone,” and walked on. Tommy never said thank you. Never called. Just walked. Like a man who’d forgotten how to ask for help, and then remembered that asking wasn’t the problem — the problem was the silence after you said it.

Old girlfriend? She was the one who called, and it cracked through the fog, and Tommy didn’t even look up, but she was the one who said he wasn’t alone anymore. Tommy didn’t know if she meant it, or if she’d just forgotten how to see him. But she saw him. That’s all that mattered.

And the car? It stayed there, just a shell on the edge of the road, frost creeping up its sides like a slow apology.

Hi, Friend,  

You know how Southwick told me about that time he saw Tommy sitting on the curb like a statue, just after the rain had stopped and the sky had turned that awful, grey-blue that makes you think the world is about to settle into a nap? Well, the story is worse than he said.  

It started on the highway at 3 a.m., rain slicking the road like a greasy mirror, headlights cutting through it like broken teeth. Tommy was there, on the shoulder, one leg giving out like a wobbly fence post. He’d slipped on the cracked pavement, and when he fell, his phone died in his pocket and the car behind him just sputtered and cut out. I was passing by, got a whiff of the cold and the wet and thought, "Well, I’ve seen enough of that to know what’s coming."  

Anyway, Southwick said he saw the ex-partner show up at a diner around 11 p.m. — just the two of them, her sitting across from Tommy, hands folded, eyes steady. She said, "I saw you on the road yesterday. I know you’re not moving." And then she handed him a sandwich, greasy and warm, and a cup of coffee, and said, "Take it. No questions. No return. Just eat." Tommy looked at it like it was a trap, then slowly opened it, took a bite. The coffee was warm in his palms — first time in days he’d touched something that didn’t feel like a memory made of ice.  

Then, at 2 a.m., fog curling around the rest stop like it was breathing, the ex-partner walks up to Tommy’s car, places a hand on the door, and says, "I remember when you said you’d never let me go. I didn’t believe you then. I don’t believe you now. But I’m here anyway." Tommy flinches, pulls his arm back, and says, "You don’t get to say that. You don’t get to remember me like that." The ex-partner doesn’t react — just stands there, silent, like the words were already written in the fog. Tommy’s hands are clenched, knuckles white, and his breath comes in short bursts.  

Then at 4 a.m., in the cracked parking lot, the ex-partner stands at the edge, holding a folded envelope with Tommy’s name on it, and says, "I’m not asking for anything. Just one thing. Will you let me know you’re here? Even if it’s just for a moment?" Tommy stares at it, then slowly reaches into his coat and pulls out a crumpled piece of paper — a note from a year ago — and hands it to her. She takes it, looks at it, then says, "I don’t need it back. I just needed to know you’d see me." Tommy doesn’t speak, just nods, and the silence between them is so heavy it feels like a stone dropped into water.  

And then, at dawn, on that lonely stretch of highway, Tommy stops at a gas station. He looks at his hands — one finger is bent at an unnatural angle, like a broken branch. He stares at it, then at the cracked asphalt, then slowly places his hand over his chest, like the body is still holding the shape of the past. He doesn’t move, doesn’t speak — just sits in the passenger seat, eyes closed, the cold of the morning seeping into his bones.  

Now, I know Southwick said he’d seen the whole thing from the side, and that the ex-partner looked like she’d been through worse things, like she’d lived a whole other life in the shadows. And Tommy? He’s still refusing help. Still. Like a man who’s decided that asking for anything is the same as giving up. But the way the ex-partner just stood there, hand on the door, and said, "I’m here anyway" — that’s the kind of thing you don’t forget.  

And Southwick? He said the way she looked at Tommy wasn’t pity, not really. It was something like a quiet acknowledgment — like she’d known the truth, and it wasn’t something that needed to be said. Just seen. Like a boat on the lake, suddenly spotted, and the crew realized they’d been watching it for days without knowing it was there.  

Funny thing: I swear, the way Tommy took the sandwich, and then the coffee, and then the hand on the door — it reminded me of when I was writing about the merkin brew and the guy said, "Gimme a firkin of merkin" and everyone laughed. But this? This is different. It’s not a joke. It’s a slow, quiet thing. Like the world is finally letting go of some damn rule it forgot it had.  

I don’t know if it’ll change anything. But I know Southwick said he’d seen the ex-partner walk away from the lot, and that she didn’t look back. Just like that. And Tommy? He’s still refusing. But now, I think the refusal is just a body remembering how to breathe.

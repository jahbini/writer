The car’s engine just sat there, dead as a snail in a drawer, the headlights still on like it was trying to remember how to breathe. I’d been sitting in the driver’s seat for twenty minutes, staring at the cracked leather and the cold vinyl of the dashboard, sipping coffee so cold it tasted like regret. The engine didn’t just stop—it just *stopped*, as if it’d decided it had enough of me, and the world beyond the windshield, which was already dark and wet, seemed to lean in and whisper, *You’re not going anywhere.* I glanced at the rearview mirror and saw my own face, pale and still, like I’d forgotten how to blink. That’s when I noticed the car had stalled on the roadside—just a few yards down the hill, where the curb dipped and the street curved like a bad memory. No one was coming. No one was going. Just the car, sitting there like a forgotten promise.

Then, from the corner of the street, a small, thin figure in a wool coat began playing a flute. Soft. Not loud, not sad, just there—like a note that had been half-forgotten and now found its way back into the air. The tune was something I’d heard once in a corner of a diner in Halifax, years ago, when I thought I’d forgotten how to listen. The flute curled around the silence, and for a moment, I thought maybe the city wasn’t so empty. Maybe help could come from a quiet place, like the way a song can reach you without a word. I watched the musician’s hands move slowly, like they were afraid of making a sound too big. It reminded me that some things don’t need to be loud to matter. I felt it in my ribs, a quiet hum, like the kind of thing you’d miss if you didn’t stop and listen.

Then—footsteps. Not loud, not hurried, just a soft *tap-tap-tap* on the wet cobblestones. I didn’t turn. I just listened. The rhythm matched the flute, almost, like someone had been walking through the dark and just decided to keep going. It made me wonder if the world was still moving, if I’d just stopped. A man, maybe, or a child, or someone just trying to get home. I thought, *What if they’d have asked for help too? What if they’d just said, “I can’t start it”?* But I didn’t. I just sat there, waiting for something to happen, and the only thing that happened was the sound of the city breathing through the wet stones. I could almost believe that if I asked, someone would answer. But I didn’t. I just let the silence grow.

Then the streetlamp flickered. Just once. A quick, sharp blink, like a heartbeat that had forgotten its rhythm. I saw it through the window, just above the curb, and it made me freeze. It wasn’t just a light—it was a mirror. It flickered like my own thoughts, stuttering, afraid to stay on. I thought, *What if I don’t deserve help? What if I’m just a man who sits in a car and waits for the world to turn?* That’s when it hit me—this wasn’t about the engine. It was about me. I’d been so used to waiting, to letting things go, that I’d forgotten how to ask. I’d forgotten that asking wasn’t weakness. It was just a way of saying, *I’m still here, and I need you to see me.*

And then—memory. Not a flash, not a shout, but a slow, cold drip of it. I was standing in the back of the station, rain soaking through my coat, and a woman with a dog had just said, “You don’t have to go alone.” I didn’t say anything. I just looked at the puddle in front of me and thought, *I could have said yes.* I could have reached out, even if it meant sounding like a fool. But I didn’t. I just walked away, and the moment passed, like a note that didn’t finish. I realized then—there was no moment to ask. It had already gone. I’d already lost it. And now, the silence wasn’t just outside—it was inside me, thick and cold, like wet wool wrapped around my chest.

So I got up. No fanfare. No hand gesture. Just a slow, deliberate turn, and I walked toward the door. I didn’t even look at the car again. I just stepped out, and the cold hit me like a slap. The wind bit at my coat, and I could feel it seep through the seams, like it knew I’d been sitting too long. I walked down the street, past the flickering streetlamp, which now seemed to pulse like a second heartbeat, slow and steady, just out of reach. The pavement was slick, the puddles reflecting the sky like broken mirrors. I passed the car, still there, engine silent, like a man who’d given up on hope. I passed the musician, who had stopped playing, and I didn’t even glance at him. I just kept walking, one step after another, each one a refusal to engage. The world was quiet, but I wasn’t. I was just moving through it, like a shadow that had forgotten how to breathe.  

And in the end, the silence between me and the world was heavier than the car’s broken engine.

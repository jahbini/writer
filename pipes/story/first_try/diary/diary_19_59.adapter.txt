The engine stopped responding to the key the moment Tommy turned it the second time. No click. No whir. Just a silence so complete it seemed to settle into the metal of the dashboard like dust. The headlights flickered once, then died, leaving the world in a cold, starless gray. Tommy didn’t move at first—just stared at the key in his palm, the cold metal pressing into his skin, the same cold that had seeped through his gloves since the moment the car stuttered on the final stretch of the road. No response. No spark. He could hear the wind, dry and thin, whistling through the cracked window, like a forgotten tune. The car was still. A small, helpless thing in the middle of the stretch of shoulder road. He breathed it in: the smell of old plastic, rubber that had dried to a brittle shell, and the faint, lingering scent of the city at night—diesel, damp concrete, a little something sweet, like forgotten gum. He thought of the old girlfriend, her laugh like a bell in the wind, and the way she used to say, “You’re always so quiet, Tommy,” and how he’d never been able to say anything back that wasn’t a lie.

Southwick appeared at the roadside two minutes later, his boots crunching the gravel like a quiet warning. He stood there with his hands in his pockets, a wool coat pulled tight against the cold, and his eyes fixed on the car as if it were a piece of art he’d forgotten how to admire. Tommy didn’t look up. He didn’t move. Southwick didn’t speak. He just stood there, his breath fogging in the air, and then, slowly, he raised his hand and pointed to the dashboard, like a man offering a silent blessing. Tommy could see the gesture, the way the sun caught the tips of Southwick’s silver hair, and for a moment, the cold of the car seemed to soften. He thought of the I Ching hexagram, the one that had been whispering through the city like a ghost in the walls—*the mountain under the mountain*, the kind that holds its breath until the wind moves. Southwick didn’t say anything. He didn’t ask if Tommy needed help. He just stood there, a silent witness, and the moment Tommy had been trying to breathe through for hours—his throat dry, his chest tight—began to slip through his fingers like sand. He could hear the way Southwick’s boots creaked, the way the wind played with the dead leaves on the shoulder, the way a crow cawed once and then stopped. The cold was dry, the kind that didn’t make you shiver but just made you feel hollow. It was contentment, in a way—clean water for all, the kind that flowed in a city that remembered its roots. Southwick was like that: a man who could sit in calm reflection at the cafe, his hands folded, eyes closed, and know the truth of the world without speaking. Tommy thought of the old girlfriend, how she used to say, “You’re so good at not saying things,” and how she’d always been right. He thought of her, and the shame came not from fear, but from the certainty that asking her would be like admitting you’d been afraid to walk in the rain all your life.

Tommy turned the key again, just to check. Nothing. He turned it once more. Nothing. The silence was louder than the sound of the city, louder than the hum of the car’s engine, louder than the memory of his first time seeing the old girlfriend at the edge of the pier, the way she laughed at his jokes, the way she said, “You’re so awkward, Tommy, you’d be better off in a painting.” He wanted to say, “I need help. I can’t get the car started.” But the words wouldn’t come. They’d been stuck in the back of his throat since the moment the engine stopped, like a cold stone. The thought of her—of her voice, of her eyes, of her knowing things he didn’t—made his chest clench. He thought of the moment when they’d broken up, when she said, “You don’t speak to me when you’re scared,” and he hadn’t answered. He didn’t want to speak. He didn’t want to speak to her. He didn’t want to speak to anyone. The moment passed. It wasn’t a shout, not a cry, not a scream. It was just a silence, a slow, quiet slipping away. The wind had changed, the air was now thicker, like it had been holding its breath. The I Ching hexagram pulsed in his bones, a quiet reminder: *the mountain under the mountain*. He looked at Southwick again, and for a moment, he saw him not as a man, but as a presence—a man who had seen too much, who had lived through too many winters, who knew the weight of silence. He didn’t move. He just stared at the car, and then slowly, slowly, he reached for the door handle. His fingers trembled. He could hear the wind, and the trees, and the way the world had gone quiet. The moment to ask passed. It didn’t come back. It just went. Like a phone call that never rang. Like a promise that never got delivered.

Tommy turned off the engine, and the silence deepened. He didn’t look back. He didn’t say anything to Southwick. He just climbed out, his legs stiff, his hands cold, and walked toward the edge of the road. The gravel underfoot was dry, cracked, and hard. He could smell the cold, the dryness, the way the earth breathed without moisture. He walked slowly, his boots crunching the stones, and the wind carried with it the scent of pine, of old wood, of something that had been forgotten. He passed a sign that said *No ABC stores*, and for a moment, it made him smile. He remembered the PNW day, the cold and wet, the way the city had felt like a prison. This was better. Cold and dry. Much better than a PNW day. He thought of the old girlfriend, of the way she’d once said, “You don’t have to talk to me, Tommy. You just have to be here.” And now, he was here. He was walking. The city was quiet. The trees were still. The sky was gray, and the stars were gone. He thought of the way the myco-probes had once told Southwick the soil was healthy, and the way the myco-minds liked being called “pond-scum” instead of “pond-dwellers.” He thought of the way the King’s Exulted Musketeers had once stopped for a secret mission at the James John Cafe, and how the King had said, “Water attacks foundations. Keep it out of the house.” He thought of the way his house was insured, and how he’d love to collect. He thought of the way the world had forgotten things, and how sometimes, the best thing you could do was just walk. He reached the edge of the road where the car had broken down, and stopped. The wind blew through the empty space, and the cold settled into his bones. He didn’t look back. He just stood there, the wind whispering through the trees, and the I Ching hexagram humming in his chest like a lullaby. He had walked home. Alone.

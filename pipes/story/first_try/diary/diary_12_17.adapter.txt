Hi, Friend,

You know how Southwick once told me about the time he saw a squirrel in a turtleneck and said it had “spiritual authority” — well, I reckon that’s the way we all are. You don’t need a squirrel, just a moment, and suddenly the world goes sideways.

So here’s what happened to Tommy. Dusk, two-lane road, rain like it’s been crying for days, the kind of gray that makes your headlights look like a bad attempt at a mood board. Tommy was just sitting there, in his old pickup, the engine sputtering like a man trying to breathe through a straw. One last hiccup, and then it died — all the lights went out, one by one, just the fuel gauge blinking red like a slow heartbeat. Tommy didn’t even move. Just leaned back, hands on the wheel, like if he kept holding on, the car might just start again. I don’t know what he thought, but the way he looked — eyes fixed, fingers curled like he’d forgotten how to uncurl — reminded me of the way Anna used to look when she was telling the story of the sailor from Turkey-Stan. “A man with a map,” she said, “and a plan to knock on doors.” Tommy didn’t have a map, just a road, and a broken engine, and a little bit of rain on his face.

Then Southwick came along. Late afternoon, sun slanting through dry grass, the kind of sun that makes you forget how cold the world can be. Southwick stopped his pickup, engine still idling, and leaned out the window with that old radio, hand-held, like he’d been practicing for a St. John’s opera. He waved, and then just handed Tommy a piece of paper — a number, a map to the tow service, “Just call it,” he said, “they’ll come in ten minutes.” Tommy stared at it like it was a cursed relic. Slowly, without touching it, he folded it back into his coat pocket. Southwick drove off, radio still playing that soft country tune, like it didn’t matter if Tommy was going to call or not. I swear, if I’d been there, I’d have said something. But I didn’t. I was just sitting in the James John Cafe, sipping a cup of coffee that tasted like regret, and I thought, “Oh, no. He didn’t need help. He needed to be alone with the silence.”

Then, Tommy met her — the old girlfriend. Dusk, under a sagging porch light, the diner cracked, half-eaten sandwich on the table. She sat across from him, coffee in hand, eyes on the road. Just like that, she said, “I remember when you said you’d never ask for help — that you’d just walk. You didn’t even try to call. You just walked.” Tommy’s jaw went stiff. He didn’t speak. He pulled out a crumpled receipt — gas station, three years ago, the one where he’d bought a gallon of diesel and walked out like a man who’d just lost a dog. He dropped it in the trash. She didn’t look at it. She just looked at him. Like she knew the way he’d kept it in his pocket, like it was a piece of paper that said “I’m not OK, but I won’t say it.”

Then Tommy walked. Dusk, gravel path down a hill, wind stirring leaves and the smell of wet earth. He stepped out of the car, boots crunching, paused, took a step forward, then another. No one said anything. No one called. Just him, walking. No look back. No sound. Just the wind picking up, the way it does when the world stops asking if you’re alright. He walked without looking, and the path wasn’t a question anymore — it was a promise.

And then, midnight, frost on the road, cold pine, distant snow. Tommy paused at a crosswalk, boots crunching on frost, looked back — just for a second — at the car behind him. But didn’t go back. Just took a step, then another, breath visible, a leaf falling from a tree. No look. No sound. Just kept walking. His hands still in his pockets, rhythm steady, like it was all part of a machine that had been running for years. He didn’t need a tow. He didn’t need help. He didn’t need anyone to know he’d been broken. He just had to keep going.

Tommy didn’t speak to Southwick after that. Not a word. He didn’t call the tow company. And I can’t say if it’s good or bad. But I can tell you this: Southwick said he’d channeled it from the Akashic Records — I think it’s true. And the way Tommy walked, like a man who’d walked through a door and didn’t come back out — well, that’s the way we all are sometimes. Not with a voice, but with a step. And sometimes, the best thing you can give is the quiet that says, “I won’t ask for help. I’ll just go.”

Funny thing — I was at the James John Cafe last week. Suzanne brought me the coffee, said “Sorry for the wait.” And Southwick fell from the ceiling, dusted himself off, and misquoted Yoda sideways: “No, there is no wait. Only enjoy. Enjoy or Enjoy NOT.” And I thought, “Yeah, but for Tommy, the only thing that mattered was the walk. Not the help. Not the pity. Just the step.”

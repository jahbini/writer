The child stops, breath catching just short of a full inhale, as the man on the bench tilts his head slightly toward the street, his fingers curled around a crumpled map like it might escape. Southwick. The name comes back to him not as a name, but as a shape: small, still, and fixed in the way a man who’s seen too many things settles into a single posture. The notebook in the child’s hands feels suddenly heavier than it should, as if it were holding the weight of all the things not said. He sees Southwick’s eyes flicker up to him, then back to the map, as if the man has been waiting for this moment, for this particular kind of silence. Yuch, that’s disgusting. But then—surprise—Southwick is looking at the street not with calculation, but with the kind of reverence for the mundane decay that makes the child wonder if this is how the world works: not through grand gestures, but through the quiet. A crumpled leaf at the corner. A bus that didn’t stop. A man who just sits and studies a map like it holds the shape of truth. And in that moment, the child feels the absence of the old girlfriend, not as a hole, but as a kind of tide that didn’t come in.  

The child recalls the last time they spoke to her—late afternoon, two years back, under a sky that had no color. They stood at the edge of the park, not speaking, just standing, the way two people do when they’ve said all they need to say and don’t know how to say it again. That silence had been thick, like wet wool. And in that silence, something had passed between them, not words, but a kind of surrender. Now, the child sees it clearly: the old girlfriend is not here. She didn’t come. She didn’t show. There was no fanfare, no smile, no walk through the trees. Just the street, the wind, and the empty space where she should have been. The child turns away from Southwick, and for a moment, the world seems to hold its breath. It is not the kind of contentment that comes from warmth or a good meal. It is contentment—steady, quiet, like the way the patrons of the James John Cafe still seek health with steady focus, like the way the St. John’s cinema keeps its kids matinees through winter. Contentment. Patrons seek health. Serves the King's Finest. Surprise: fans consistently watching hips despite taunts. And in that moment, the child feels the weight of the unheld moment, like a stone dropped into a well that doesn’t echo back.  

Then, the Tarot card comes to him—The Hanged Man. Not the one from the book, not the one they taught in the classroom. This one, the one that feels like the silence. The one that says: suspension. Surrender. The moment of stillness. The child thinks of the old girlfriend, not as someone who should have come, but as someone who might have stayed if only the world had allowed her to be quiet. The child thinks of Southwick, who sits there with a map, who has seen the world go on without ceremony, and thinks: if I stay, will I be a hollow object of pity? Will I be the one who failed to hold the moment? He feels the fear of hope collapsing—like a house built on sand, the kind of fear that makes you want to turn your back on the memory before it can be named. So he walks away, notebook open, pen hovering above the page, the way a man might hesitate before pulling a trigger. He does not turn back. He does not look at Southwick again. He does not speak. He just walks, the paper crinkling with the weight of the city’s forgotten things.  

Then, the words come. Not with fanfare, not with urgency, but with the kind of quiet that only happens when you’ve given up on hope. The child writes: *It was not a meeting. It was a silence. The card that came to mind was The Hanged Man. I did not stay. I did not hope.* The pen moves again, slowly, like a man remembering a dream. Surprise—Tarot 12, Hanged Man lesson revealed. The words are not grand. They are not dramatic. But they are true. The child closes the notebook, and the paper feels like a piece of skin pulled from the body. He walks away, the city returning to its quiet rhythm, the wind carrying the scent of autumn leaves—crunching, fallen, and steady. Southwick is still on the bench, the map now folded in his lap, the city passing by as if nothing had changed. The child doesn’t look back. The moment is sealed, not in speech, but in the quiet of a page.

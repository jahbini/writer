Hi, Friend,

You know, I was walking through the St. John’s fog this morning, sipping a lukewarm chamomile that tastes like forgotten promises, when I thought I’d tell you about Tommy. I didn’t know if it was a good idea, but Southwick told me it happened in a way that makes you wonder if we all have a memory of a walk we never took — and I can’t stop thinking about it now, so here goes.

It was a rain-slicked downtown at 6:17, gray and cold, puddles reflecting neon signs like broken mirrors. Tommy was just standing there, stopped at the corner of 5th and Ash, like a man who’d forgotten how to move through the world. He wasn’t looking for anything — just watching a man in a red coat lean against a lamppost, waiting for a bus. Tommy watched him for 45 seconds, then walked forward, his foot tapping once on the wet pavement — then again — then stopped, breathing slow and shallow, like his body remembered a rhythm it hadn’t taken in years. I don’t know if it was the rain or the way the puddles looked like mirrors, but it felt like something was trying to speak through the water. Tommy didn’t look at the red coat after that, just kept walking, like the world had forgotten he existed. 

Then, at 7:03, golden hour, the air still and warm, the scent of cut grass and distant traffic — I saw the ex-partner sitting on a bench, hands folded, eyes closed, like she was waiting for something to come back. Tommy saw her for the first time in two years. He stopped two paces away, his foot hovering, and she opened her eyes, smiled slightly, and said, “You don’t have to walk home alone.” Tommy’s jaw tightened, his hands clenching at his sides, and he didn’t move, but his feet twitched once, like a ghost remembering how to walk without fear. I was standing behind a shuttered bookstore, pretending to look at the sign “Used Books — Last Copy of 1973 Poetry of the Wind,” but I swear I felt the heat of her words like a physical burn. It wasn’t pity, not in the way we’d always thought — just a quiet, old acknowledgment of something that had been there, long before either of us thought it was gone.

Then, at 8:45, in a narrow alley behind the bookstore, dark and damp, with a single streetlight casting a shaky glow, Southwick appeared. He was holding a clipboard, a folded map, and said, “I know this route — would you like me to walk with you?” Tommy’s eyes narrowed, his hands tightened around his coat, and he took a step back, then another, until his body was pressed against the wall. His foot lifted, dropped, and his breath came in short bursts, like his lungs were trying to remember how to breathe without resistance. Southwick just stood there, silent, and Tommy finally said, “No,” in a whisper so soft it was almost a sigh. Southwick didn’t push, didn’t smile — just nodded, like he’d known all along that some things are meant to be carried alone. I don’t know if he saw it happen, but I know he saw it happen — and he said it was like the way a house remembers a door that was never opened. 

Then, at 10:12, the rain had picked up, the streetlights flickered, and the air smelled like wet concrete and old paper. Tommy reached the top step and stopped, his hand on the railing, as if the building itself was holding its breath. His body started moving in slow, deliberate patterns — a step, a pause, a step that mirrored the rhythm of the walk he used to take with the ex-partner. His left foot lifted, then dropped, and his right followed, like his body had forgotten how to walk, and now had to relearn it. He didn’t look at the door, didn’t open it — just walked up the last step, his body now moving like a ghost of a walk that never ended. I saw him go through the front door, and for a second, I thought I saw him hesitate at the threshold — but then he just walked in, like the house had always known how to be quiet with him. 

Tommy’s finger is still bent at an unnatural angle, and I saw it when he passed the alley. Southwick didn’t mention it, but I think he knows. The ex-partner didn’t say anything after she left — just walked away without looking back, like she’d known the moment would come. She didn’t pity him, not in the way we all think we’d be expected to. She just acknowledged that the walk was gone, and the walk was never meant to end. Tommy doesn’t talk about it, but I saw him in the park the other day, sitting by the river, looking at his shoes, like they were still a part of a story he’d never finished. 

And Southwick? Well, he’s the one who told me about the way the soil remembers. He said the myco-minds under the city are happy with the extra water, but dislike being called pond-scum. I thought, “That’s a damn good metaphor for what Tommy’s body feels like now.”

Scene:  
Sunrise at the corner of Blyth and Union, the kind of morning where the air tastes like wet pavement and old coffee. The street’s still half-dreaming, the bus stop’s got a single yellow bench, one of those with a crack in the seat like a dried riverbed. I’m standing there with a thermos of tea—stale, but still warm—and a paper bag of bagels, the kind with a little bit of cheese and a whisper of onion. The sky’s just beginning to blush, and the pigeons are already arguing over a crumb. I don’t know why I’m here, just that the wind’s not blowing, and the world feels like it’s holding its breath. Quiet, in a way that doesn’t feel empty—just… present. Like the city’s finally stopped pretending it’s busy.

Arrival:  
I walk in the door and the bell jingles—old, rusty, like it’s been trying to get noticed for years. The shop’s small, shelves stacked with jars of pickles, bottles of honey, and a few mismatched mugs with names like “Sister’s 1983” and “For the Rain.” The owner, a woman with silver curls and a red apron, looks up and just nods. I don’t say much, just hand her the bagels and say, “You don’t have to sell me anything, just… let me know if you’re open.” She smiles and says, “Always,” and hands me a cup of chamomile, steaming, like it’s been waiting. My hands are trembling a little—probably from the cold, or the way my shoes creak like old hinges. I can smell the wood, the oil, the faintest hint of turmeric. I don’t know what I’m supposed to be doing here, but I don’t want to go back.

Disturbance:  
Then the lights go out. Not just a flicker—full, sudden, like the city’s been punched in the chest. I’m standing by the counter, the bagels forgotten, and the lights are gone. For a second, I swear I hear the fridge hum, but it’s just silence. Then a crackle from the back—someone’s turning on a generator, or maybe the power’s coming back through a different wire. I turn and see the woman at the far shelf, her hand on a metal box, whispering something in a language I don’t know. I don’t move. My breath catches, and the smell of wet wool and burnt toast fills the air. I don’t know if it’s the dark or the way she’s holding the box like it’s a child, but my chest tightens. I think I hear a noise like a door closing, but I can’t be sure. It’s just that for a moment, the world feels like it’s folding in on itself.

Reflection:  
I sit at the table by the window, the one with the chipped blue paint, and look at the paper bag. It’s still warm. I don’t know if I bought anything. I don’t know if I even wanted to. I just remember the way the tea tasted, how the bagels were still soft, and how the woman said, “You come back every week, don’t you?” I don’t know what she meant. I don’t know if I do. I just remember the silence after the lights went out, and how it wasn’t empty—just different. Like the world had paused, and I’d been walking through it with my eyes half-closed, not really seeing, and now I’m here, blinking, and suddenly it all feels like it’s been there all along.

Realization:  
I didn’t come for anything. I just came because the place felt like a mirror—small, worn, and quiet. I didn’t need to buy a thing, or talk to anyone. I just needed to stand in a room where the light doesn’t rush, where the air doesn’t shout. I left the bagels on the counter. I didn’t eat them. I just watched the steam rise and curl, like a slow apology. And for the first time in a while, I didn’t feel like I had to explain myself. Just stood there, with a cup of tea and a quiet hum in the walls, and realized: sometimes, the thing you’re looking for isn’t found—it’s just remembered. And I’d been forgetting how to remember.

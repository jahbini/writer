Hi, Friend,  

You know how Southwick always says he’s got a sixth sense for the quiet things — like when the world stops breathing and you just know something’s off? Well, I was just sitting at the James John Cafe, sipping my lukewarm tea (the one with the tiny hula dancer on the lighter, which I swear still has a bit of ginger in it), when the story came up again. It’s one of those things that slips into your head like a bad memory you didn’t think you had — Tommy’s car, you know, the one that’s been broken for a while now, just sitting there like a forgotten promise.  

It was a quiet dusk, rain pooling in the gutters like slow, dark breaths, the kind of night where the streetlight flickers off and the silence stretches so long it feels like someone turned down the volume on the world. Tommy was just there, leaning against the car’s door, fingers pressing into the rusted frame, eyes fixed on the cracked windshield where the rain streaked in jagged lines. I didn’t think much of it at first — just another guy with a broken machine, refusing help like he’s always done.  

Then Southwick came by, that old fellow, always with that faded blue coat, coming out of his bakery, the neon sign flickering ‘SOUTHWICK’S BAKED GOODS’ like it was trying to say something important. He stopped, looked at the car, then at Tommy, who didn’t budge. “You know,” he said, calm as ever, “I used to drive a ’98 Camry — same model. I’d say it’s just a matter of time before it stops. You don’t have to be alone with it.”  

Tommy didn’t answer, but his hands twitched — just a little, like a memory of when he used to say, “I don’t need help then, and I don’t need it now,” and Southwick had said it like it was a secret. That night, the rain slowed to a drizzle, the streetlamp glowing dimly, and Southwick walked back into the bakery, the door closing behind him. Tommy watched it shut, then turned to the car, hands tightening around the edge of the door. “I didn’t need help then,” he whispered, “and I don’t need it now.” Then — just a second — the ex-partner came to mind, the way she’d said, “You’re just afraid to be seen as broken,” and he flinched like he’d been struck. Breath caught. Then he walked slowly toward the end of the street, not looking back.  

By dawn, the rain had stopped, the world still wet and quiet, and Tommy stepped out of the car, boots crunching on gravel. He walked slow, one foot in front of the other, hands in his pockets, the wind lifting the edge of his coat like the world was breathing with him. For the first time, the path ahead wasn’t a place of waiting — it was a destination. He walked past the streetlight, into the dark, and the car was behind him, a silent, immovable shadow.  

I know, I know — I’m telling you this like it’s a fairy tale, but it’s just the way things are in St. John’s. Southwick never says much, but he’s always there, like a man who’s seen the quiet things. And Tommy? Well, he’s always had that way of refusing help, like it’s the only way he can keep his own skin on. I wonder if the ex-partner still knows how he looked when he turned away from the car — afraid, maybe, but not afraid of being seen.  

And you know what? I don’t know if it meant anything. Maybe it was just a moment. But Southwick said it was like the way the world stops, and you know something’s wrong — and then it keeps going, like it’s just forgotten.

Southwick sat in the passenger seat, the engine silent as if it had forgotten how to breathe. The key turned in the ignition, cold and unyielding, and nothing happened—no click, no sputter, no groan of metal under strain. The road ahead stretched into a black seam, unmarked and unlit, and the only sound was the slow, hollow tick of the windshield wipers, which had long since ceased to be useful. Southwick could smell the dry paper of the glove compartment and the faint, sour tang of old petrol that had seeped from the floorboards into the carpet, a smell that reminded him of the old shed behind the garage, where the generator had once died in the rain. Tommy sat beside him, hands folded on his knees, eyes fixed on the cracked leather of the seat, as if he could will the engine back into life with just one glance.

Southwick looked at Tommy then. “We don’t deserve this,” he said, voice low and flat, like a sentence written in pencil on a dry wall. The words came out before he thought them, and the silence that followed was heavier than the engine’s failure. He could feel the weight of them settle in his chest, like a stone pulled from a river and dropped into the mud. Tommy didn’t move. Southwick thought of the way Old friend had once told him, years back, that the most important things in life were not what happened, but what you decided not to take. He didn’t believe it then, and now, in the cold stillness of the car, he could feel it again—the way the world had grown quiet, and the only thing louder than the silence was the truth that the good things, the ones that should be easy, were never meant for people like them. Tommy sat there, eyes down, like a man who’d just realized he’d been carrying a sack of wet laundry and didn’t know it.

Tommy reached for the door handle, fingers brushing the metal, then stopped. His hand hovered, trembling slightly, as if the movement would break something. Southwick watched it happen—just a twitch, a pause—and felt the air between them turn thick, like syrup left too long in a glass. Tommy’s lips parted, and Southwick saw the thought form, then dissolve, like smoke from a match that hadn’t been struck. He didn’t say it out loud, but the moment passed—Tommy’s hand slipped back, and the engine stayed silent, as if it had already decided to give up. The kind of silence that doesn’t just mean no noise, but that something is missing, something that should have been said, should have been done, should have been believed in. Southwick didn’t say it either, because in that moment, the thought of asking for help wasn’t just unpalatable—it was like asking for a handout from a man who had already forgotten how to give anything.

Southwick got out of the car and walked toward the road, his boots crunching on the gravel like stones under a blade. The car sat there, engine dark, lights off, like a body that had stopped breathing and forgotten how to move. He didn’t look back. Tommy stayed in the seat, his fingers twitching again, but Southwick didn’t say anything, didn’t offer a word of reassurance. He walked with his shoulders low, the wind catching his coat like a tattered flag. The road ahead was still dark, but now it wasn’t just the absence of light that bothered him—it was the way the dark had filled the space between them, and the way it had grown heavier, heavier than the silence, heavier than the engine’s failure. The only thing louder than the engine was the silence between them, and that silence now carried the echo of something that had never been said: that maybe they didn’t need help. Maybe they were just meant to walk through the dark, alone, and learn how to carry the weight of the world without anyone else.

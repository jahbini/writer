The streetlamp flickers and the road *wobbles*, like a mirror held too long in cold hands. I’m walking down Water Street in the evening, boots sinking slightly into the wet cobblestones, the pavement reflecting the streetlights like oil on water—shifting, rippling, wrong. The lights don’t just fall; they *breathe*. One moment the crosswalk is straight ahead, the next it’s curved like a question mark, and I’m standing there, blinking, as if the city forgot how to grow straight lines. I stop. The notebook in my hand feels heavier than it should. I open it, and the page is blank except for a single line in the corner, written in a shaky hand: *Do I deserve to be found again?* I don’t know who wrote it. I don’t know if it’s me. But the wind carries the scent of wet paper and old rain, and the street feels like it’s trying to tell me something it’s never been allowed to say.

The neighbor stands there, a man with a garden trowel in his hand, just behind the corner of the corner shop. He doesn’t move. I look at him, and for a second, I think he’s going to say something—something soft, something like *you’ve been here before, haven’t you?*—but instead, he just watches me, like I’m a ghost with a flashlight. I open the notebook wider, and whisper the question into the dark: *Is this the way home?* He flinches, just slightly, as if the wind had caught his sleeve and pulled it. Then he starts to speak—soft, hesitant, like a sentence that’s been folded and forgotten for years. “You used to say Water Street was the one that remembered,” he begins, voice thin, like old thread. “Like it knew when you were sad or when you were happy.” I don’t reply. I just watch him, and the words hang like wet laundry in the air. He trails off, then turns, and walks back into the shop, not looking at me, not looking at the notebook. I don’t know if he meant to say more. I don’t know if he meant to say anything at all. But the silence after is the one thing that feels like it’s been missing for years—like a door that stayed ajar, just long enough to let the wind in.

Then, in the margin of the notebook, I see it—*The Hanged Man*. Not drawn, not painted, just there, in ink, its shadow stretching across the wet pavement like a ghost with a purpose. The card’s head is tilted, eyes half-closed, and the rope at its waist is a thin line, but it stretches out, long and thin, across the wet stone. I blink. The wind picks up again, and the streetlamp flickers once more, and the road *pulls*—it tilts, and for a moment, I see it as it was when we were young, when we thought the world could be simple, when we thought love was just a line drawn in the sand. I look at the card again. The Hanged Man. I know what it means. It’s not about being found. It’s about being *let go*. About not knowing if you’re still alive when you’re suspended. And I—*I*—don’t believe I’m meant to be found. I don’t believe I’m meant to be forgiven. I don’t believe I’m meant to be remembered. So the neighbor, who just tried to speak, turns and walks toward the next block, and when he sees a student leaning against a lamppost, reading a book with a sketchy cover, he stops. He doesn’t say anything. He just points at the notebook and says, “She asked if this was the way home.” The student looks up, nods slowly, and says, “Yeah, it looks like it.” And that’s it. No name. No history. Just a moment, like a ripple in a puddle, and the student walks on, not knowing who she was, not knowing what it meant. The notebook stays open, the card still there, the shadow still stretching, like it’s trying to tell me something I didn’t ask for.

I stand at the crosswalk now, my boots still wet, the notebook still open, the question still there. I don’t speak. I don’t move. I just stand, watching the student’s shadow fade into the street, and the streetlamp flickers one last time—just once—like a breath, like a sigh. I don’t go back. I don’t ask. I don’t need to. The road is still unfamiliar. The card is still there. And I know, in the quiet, that I didn’t speak because I didn’t believe I deserved to be heard. Not by her. Not by him. Not by anyone. And maybe that’s the point. Maybe the road only flickers when we’re not ready to walk. Maybe the Hanged Man isn’t a warning. Maybe it’s just a reminder that sometimes, the most important thing we can give is silence—and that silence, when it’s earned, is the only thing that feels like peace. I turn and walk past the crosswalk, not looking back, and the street feels less like a path and more like a memory—wet, slow, and full of things I never said.

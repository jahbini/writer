The Car That Would Not Start

Tommy sat in the car, hands curled around the cold metal of the door handle, the engine silent as a grave. No hum, no click, no whisper of the fuel pump — just stillness, thick and suffocating, like the air after a door closes behind you. The headlights, once a promise of direction, had gone dark hours ago, and the dashboard was a lifeless sheet of plastic. He tried the key again, the way he had done a hundred times before, fingers trembling slightly, the way they always did when the problem wasn’t just mechanical but personal. The car would not start. It wouldn’t budge, wouldn’t cough, wouldn’t even breathe. He could feel the cold seep into his palms, the way it always did when the world stopped responding. Outside, the wind carried the dry, clean scent of winter pine — cold and dry, much better than a PNW day, he thought, though he didn’t say it aloud. The road stretched ahead like a long, unbroken line of gray, and the silence in the car felt like a mirror. He could hear the creak of the metal under the weight of his own stillness, and in that moment, the I Ching hexagram — the one that had been whispering in his bones all morning — seemed to settle, a quiet presence, like frost on glass.

Southwick appeared at the roadside five minutes later, a man with a wool coat pulled tight around his shoulders, his hands in his pockets, eyes scanning the stretch of asphalt. He stood there for a long moment, his shadow stretching long and thin, like a bow drawn across a string. Tommy watched him from the window, the glass fogging slightly with his breath. Southwick didn’t move toward the car, didn’t wave, didn’t speak — just stood there, a quiet monument to the moment. Tommy considered whether to speak. Just one sentence. “I think the car won’t start,” he could say. “Could you help me?” The thought was a weight in his chest, like a stone pulled from the river. Southwick was known to be practical, not sentimental, and Tommy knew that if he said it, Southwick would look at him with a kind of weary understanding, then maybe help. But the moment passed. It wasn’t the right moment. He didn’t speak. The cold wind snaked through the gaps in the car’s body, and the silence grew heavier, like a blanket pulled too tight. Southwick just stood there, a figure against the gray, his face unreadable, his hands still in his pockets. The moment to ask passed. Tommy didn’t move. He didn’t even look at the man again. He just sat, the cold seeping into his legs, the engine’s absence a kind of gravity he couldn’t outrun.

Tommy’s mind raced, not with the engine’s failure but with the memory of the old girlfriend — her laugh like wind chimes, her hands always warm, the way she used to say, “You always just sit there, like a statue.” He remembered her voice, the way she’d say, “I know you don’t like asking for help, Tommy, but sometimes it’s the only way to get the answer.” And he thought of her now — not just the way she had been kind, but how she had known him, how she had seen the cracks in him without flinching. The shame was a cold knot in his stomach, the pride that he couldn’t ask for help like a child asking for a cookie. He thought of her in the café, the way she’d once said, “You don’t have to be strong to be kind. Just be real.” And then, the fear: what if she’d say, “You were so proud, you forgot how to ask?” Tommy had never told her the truth about the car, about the silence, about the way the world had stopped working when the world stopped being kind. He thought of the way she’d once laughed at his silence, like it was a joke, and then he realized — that was the moment he’d been avoiding. The moment to ask passed. The wind howled, the sky cracked with a thin, gray light, and Tommy stayed in the car, paralyzed by the shame of not speaking, by the pride of not begging for help, by the way the I Ching hexagram pulsed beneath his ribs like a slow, steady heartbeat.

Tommy finally got out, the door groaning open like a wound that had been open too long. He didn’t look back. The cold of the road seeped into his boots, the soles stiff and numb. He walked with his shoulders hunched, his hands tucked into his pockets, the way he had when he’d walked home after the argument with the old girlfriend, after he’d promised himself he wouldn’t ask for anything. He passed Southwick, who now stood just a few yards away, his coat flapping slightly in the wind. Tommy didn’t speak. He didn’t even look at him. He just kept walking, the pavement beneath him crunching underfoot like broken glass. He could smell the wet earth, the pine, the cold air — clean, sharp, like the way the world had smelled before the storms, before the world got too busy with its own noise. He thought of the way the myco-Minds under the soil had liked the extra water, though they disliked being called “pond-scum.” He thought of the old girlfriend, and how she had once said, “You don’t have to fix everything, Tommy. Just let it breathe.” He thought of the way the world had ignored the cracks, like the way the homeless rode buses just to get warm, like the way the world had forgotten the foundation of things. He thought of the joy in the realization: we had compassion too, and we had been complacent. And in that moment, the wind carried the scent of the lake — a boat on the lake was suddenly spotted, and the crew waved, and Tommy saw it, just for a moment, the best view in St. John’s.

Hi, Friend,  

You know how Southwick’s got this way of appearing in the most inconvenient places, like a badger in a tea party, just when you’re about to drop a damn meteorite into the void? Well, this one’s got the fog of a bad memory, and the kind of rain that sticks to your coat like a secret you’re not supposed to know.  

It happened on a dusk that smelled like wet paper and old fish, the kind of night when the headlights don’t work and you feel like the world is just breathing out of your ears. Tommy was there, meandering down that two-lane stretch, the kind of road that only shows its teeth in bad weather. The engine coughed once, like a dying animal that’s forgotten its name, then just… stopped. Battery light blinking red, like a little child with a broken toy. Tommy didn’t fumble for the radio. Didn’t call anyone. Just leaned his forehead into the cold glass, and stared at the windshield as if it had a map to somewhere else he’d forgotten how to get back to.  

Then, just past the spot, under a gray sky that didn’t want to lift its hand, a red pickup sat there, white cross on the side, like something out of a hymnal. Southwick was in it, all denim and silence, leaning out, hand open and calm. No words, just a little gesture — points at the car, like “You’ve been sitting here too long.” Tommy didn’t blink. Didn’t move. Just stared at the cross, like it meant something he’d been told not to believe in. Southwick didn’t push. Didn’t say “Get in. I’ll drive you home.” Just kept it simple, like the whole thing was a line in a song he’d forgotten the title to.  

Then, in the dark, just after midnight, the radio in a car parked behind him went to life — old girlfriend’s voice, faint and distorted, like a ghost trying to say something important. “You don’t have to do this alone,” she said. Tommy’s chest went tight like leather being pulled over a nail. He turned his head slowly, saw nothing but the dark, and then turned back. His hands were white on the wheel, knuckles bleeding, and for a full minute, he didn’t breathe. Like if he said anything, his body would dissolve into a puddle under the road.  

Dawn came at 5:42, sun rising like a promise that had been forgotten. The engine was silent, the keys cold in his hand, the radio dead. Tommy took a step forward, then another, foot brushing the wet edge, and didn’t look back. He didn’t even check his phone, or his map. Just walked. His left hand went to his belt, then stopped. No phone, no signal, just the motion of walking. And then, for the first time in a long time, his body moved forward without hesitation. Like the weight of it had been carried inside for too long, and finally, finally, released.  

I don’t know if it’s the best thing that ever happened, or if it’s just the way things fall apart and come back together. But Southwick saw it, just a bit. Old girlfriend’s voice, even if it was just a whisper through the static, meant something. And Tommy? He didn’t need to say anything. He just walked.

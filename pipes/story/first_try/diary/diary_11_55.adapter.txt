Dusk on the cracked asphalt of Route 12, wind snatching at the rusted fenders of Tommy’s old sedan, rain pooling in the front grille like dark oil. The engine sputters one last time, then dies with a groan that echoes in the hollow of the car’s frame. I lean over the wheel, fingers trembling on the cold metal, and watch the dashboard lights blink out one by one. I touch the gearshift, then the brake, then the door handle — all still warm, all still inert. My need to move is now entirely dependent on the car’s mechanical failure. No engine means no movement. I don’t touch the door handle, not even to close it, as if the gesture would mean surrendering control to the vehicle’s failure. I stand in the driver’s seat, legs still folded, eyes fixed on the cracked windshield, as if the car’s silence confirms my own stillness. The rain doesn’t fall on me — it just falls, and the puddles ripple like the slow breath of something that’s forgotten how to speak.

Late afternoon on a dry, sun-baked stretch of county road, the sky a pale blue with no clouds, the wind still, the air thick with dust and heat. Southwick, a man in a faded denim jacket and a pickup truck, pulls over just past the broken stop sign. He leans out, opens the truck door, and hands me a spare key with a grin. 'Just a little spark, pal. I’ve seen this before. You just need to turn it on, not ask for help.' I stare at the key, then at Southwick’s eyes — steady, kind, unbothered — and for a second, my hand drifts toward it, then stops. The opportunity to restart the car is now physically available — a direct, non-judgmental path to mobility. My body resists the movement of reaching for the key, as if the gesture would confirm my helplessness. I stand frozen in the middle of the road, the key in my palm, my breath shallow, as if the offer has made the silence between us heavier than the heat. I don’t know why I don't take it. Maybe because the way Southwick says it makes it sound like I’ve been doing this all my life, like I just forgot how to ask for help, like I forgot how to be seen.

Dusk, under a low-hanging gray sky, the road slick with dew and the smell of wet earth, a single streetlight flickering at the edge of the road. My phone buzzes. Old girlfriend texts: 'I saw you at the stop. You’re not alone. I’m coming over.' I stand at the edge of the road, staring at the cracked tire, and suddenly drop the spare key into the mud. I turn and walk backward, slowly, toward the car, then stop, and when I look back, my hands are clenched into fists, knuckles white. 'I don’t need anyone,' I mutter, 'I just need to be alone.' The need to restart the car is physically unmet — the key is lost, the car remains dead, and the opportunity is now gone. The protection grows louder than the urgency: I don’t just avoid asking — I destroy the possibility of it with my body. The wind carries the words of the past into my bones — the way she used to laugh when we were young, the way she’d say, 'You're so stubborn, you’d drive a car into a lake just to prove you didn’t need a tow truck.' I don’t need anyone. I don’t need anyone. I just need to be alone. The silence between me and the car is now louder than the engine’s failure.

Dawn, the sky still dark, the road glistening with dew, the air cool and still, the first light creeping over the hills like a slow breath. My foot touches the pavement — the first time in hours I have stepped outside the car’s shadow. I lift my foot, then place it on the cracked asphalt, and take a step forward. My shoulders straighten. My eyes go forward. No one is there, no one is watching, and the car is now a silent, distant thing behind me. The need for mobility through external help is now obsolete — movement is initiated without any vehicle or assistance. My body moves in response to internal resolve, not external prompts. The walk begins under conditions of no external support. I don’t look back. I don’t feel the weight of the key in my hand anymore. I feel the cold of the dew on my feet, the way the dew feels like a memory of rain, like the way I used to feel when I was ten and my boots were wet after school. I walk, one foot in front of the other, my body now moving without a vehicle, the rhythm of my step steady and unbroken.

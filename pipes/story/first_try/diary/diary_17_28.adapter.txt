Hi, Friend,  

So here’s what I heard — not from Tommy himself, but from the way Southwick told it, and the way the old girlfriend’s voice bounced around like a wet sock in a hallway. Tommy’s car just… gave up. On the shoulder, 3:17 a.m., rain slick as a bad memory, fog curling like smoke from the asphalt, headlights slicing through it like a surgeon’s knife. He was just standing there, not even breathing, eyes fixed on that cracked windshield where the rain streaks looked like veins. He tried the key again — starter clicked, nothing. Then he slammed the door shut, like it was the last thing he’d be able to do, and just leaned against the hood, hands trembling, like he’d forgotten how to shake them.  

Then, 4:03 a.m., the fog lifting just enough to see the pickup truck, red light blinking like a bad omen. Southwick steps out, no fuss, just a man in a worn denim jacket, the kind that’s seen more than it should have, and hands Tommy a folded paper with a number. “Just call me if you need anything,” he says, calm as a fish in a still pond. “I’ve been down this road before. I know where the rest stop is.” Tommy stares at it, then at Southwick’s eyes — steady, not a twitch, like he’d seen it all. Tommy’s fingers twitch near the glove compartment, but don’t reach. Like he’s afraid the moment will turn into a confession.  

Then, 5:45 a.m., sun breaking through, the fog gone, just the world dry and cracked, a leaf floating in the wind. That’s when the old girlfriend’s voice comes — not loud, just echoing from the edge of the road, like a memory that didn’t want to die. “You don’t have to do this alone, Tommy,” she says, “I know you can’t handle it, but I can help.” She’s there, holding a folded map, like she’s been waiting for that moment for years. Tommy turns, eyes wide, steps back into the car, hand reaches for the door handle — then stops. Fingers curl into fists. “I don’t need anyone to see me like this,” he whispers. Like he’s already convinced the world’s gonna judge him for the way he stands. The map stays in her hands, like it’s a secret.  

And then, 6:12 a.m., sun high, air still, cracked asphalt like old skin. A gust of wind lifts a piece of gravel, and Tommy’s foot slips slightly as he steps off the edge. He takes a slow step away — heel presses into the ground, just one foot in front of the other. Doesn’t look back. Doesn’t say anything. Just walks. The wind brushes his sleeves like a whisper, and for a moment, the world is quiet enough that you can hear the way the pavement creaks under his weight.  

I know Southwick says he channeled it from the Akashic Records — I think it has a message for our times, maybe. And the old girlfriend? She’s been gone a long time, but her voice still shows up in the corners of things, like a shadow that won’t leave. Tommy’s not the one who’s breaking down — it’s the way he won’t ask for help, even when Southwick hands him a number like it’s a key to a safe. And when the old girlfriend shows up, it’s like she’s not trying to fix it, just to say, “I know you’re not alone.”  

Funny, the way things go — like we all have these little things that happen in the middle of the night, just outside the light, and we don’t want to admit they’re real.

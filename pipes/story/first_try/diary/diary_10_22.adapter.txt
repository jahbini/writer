The wind came in like it had been holding its breath all day, and when it finally broke, it didn’t just blow—it *sang*. I stood just past the corner of Water and Third, watching her walk, the notebook open in her hands like a prayer she didn’t know how to say. She’d been here before, once, when we used to walk this stretch with our hands in our pockets and our shoulders slumped like we’d already decided we’d never get anywhere. Now, she stood still, notebook open, eyes fixed on the street ahead, and asked, “Do you think we were meant to be?” — not to me, not to anyone. Just out loud, like the wind was the only witness. The pavement shimmered under the streetlights, wet and cracked, and the wind hummed through the cracks of the buildings, as if the city itself was trying to remember a name it’d forgotten.

Then, from a little ways down, a guitar began—soft, just a single note, like a finger tapping the edge of a drum. It matched the rhythm of her heartbeat, slow and uneven, the way it used to when we were both too tired to talk and just wanted the silence to have meaning. Her footsteps echoed on the wet cobblestones, each one a tiny splash in the dark, and for a moment it felt like we were both walking backwards through time. The streetlamp above her flickered—just once, then again—and in that second, the reflection of her face twisted, stretched, like a photo taken from the side of a cracked mirror. She didn’t recognize it. Her eyes widened, and for a split second, the path ahead looked like it was bending into itself, folding over like a map left in the rain. Then she said it, quiet as a whisper: “It doesn’t look like Water Street anymore.” Her voice cracked, and the wind took it, carried it off like a piece of paper in a storm.

She stepped back, one foot behind the other, and the street seemed to lean in, as if it were trying to swallow her. The reflection in the streetlight now looked like a woman with too many shadows, one arm outstretched, the other folded like a letter never sent. I didn’t move. I just stood there, like a man who’d seen too many things come and go, and the silence between us grew heavier than the wind. I could have stepped in, said something—something soft, something like *you were never lost, you were just forgotten*—but I didn’t. I watched. I watched her eyes, wide and unblinking, as if the world had gone silent and only she could hear the echo of a name she’d once said to me in a kitchen with too much light.  

Then, from the door across the way, a man leaned in, his hands on the wood, his eyes fixed on her. He didn’t speak. Just leaned in, and began to repeat the story—word for word, as if he’d memorized it from a dream. “You said we were meant to be,” he said, voice flat, like he was reading from a script. “You said we were meant to be, and then you walked away, and I didn’t know what to do. You just… walked.” He didn’t pause. Didn’t look at her. Just kept going, as if the words were already written in the cracks of the sidewalk. And when he’d finished, he turned, not looking back, and walked down the street, past the corner, past the street musician, who had stopped playing, and into the night, like the story had never been his to carry.  

She stood there, still, and looked up at the streetlight again. There, in the flicker, the Tarot card of The Hanged Man appeared—face down, but still there, its edges sharp as a blade. She didn’t touch it. She didn’t believe it. She didn’t believe the good thing was theirs to take. She just looked at it, like it was a ghost trying to say something it couldn’t say, and walked on—past the intersection, toward the corner, but not with the sense of relief. Just a slow, steady step, like a door that had been left ajar, and she’d forgotten how to close it.

Southwick sat in the passenger seat, hands folded over the cold metal of the door, eyes fixed on the wet road ahead. The wind came down Water Street like a slow, cold breath, carrying with it the smell of rain-wet concrete and distant fish markets. The pavement reflected the streetlights—flickering, watery, rippling like oil on water—and the soft, melancholy notes of a street musician played from somewhere just beyond the corner, a tune that didn’t belong to any known song, just to the moment. Footsteps on wet cobblestones echoed behind them, a sound that hadn’t come from the street, but from the city itself, remembering its own rhythm. The streetlamp flickered once, then steadied, its light spilling across the puddles in broken arcs. Southwick pointed toward the harbor, his finger lingering just above the glove compartment, as if the water below might answer him. He tilted his head skeptically, as if the city itself were pretending to listen. His fingers drummed lightly on the armrest—soft, like a nervous habit—and then, slowly, he rubbed his temples, the motion so small it might have been a memory of a time when he’d done it in a hospital waiting room. He watched people outside, their faces blurred by the rain, their gestures mechanical, their words lost to the noise. Crowds believed headlines more than events, and Southwick saw it in the way they stared at their phones, not at each other. Arguments circled like gulls, always returning to the same point, always repeating, and he thought of how the easiest thought was the borrowed one, how thinking was harder than repeating. The silence of the car grew louder than the wind, and the world outside felt increasingly distant. The engine had given one final, weak click and then remained silent, as if it had known its time had come. Southwick sighed deeply, a sound like stone settling into water, and then leaned back heavily, the motion slow and deliberate. He finished his coffee, the cup now cold, the taste sour, and he looked at Tommy, who fidgeted in his seat, eyes darting to the rearview mirror, as if the world were behind him. The wind picked up, and the street musician stopped playing, as if the city had taken a breath.  

A passing figure in a coat moved slowly down the street, shoulders hunched, a hat pulled low. Southwick saw it and froze. The old friend—his face softened by time, his eyes kind, his coat the same woolen grey from twenty years ago—passed by, slow and sure, as if the world had paused for him. For a moment, Southwick felt the weight of that memory, the way they used to walk through the city, not in cars, not in silence, but in conversation, in laughter, in the belief that the world could be changed. The old friend had once said, “We don’t need new things, just new ways of seeing the old ones.” Now, Southwick felt the same thing: habits build invisible prisons, and habits shape beliefs quietly. History mostly repeated emotions, and human beings repeated themselves, not because they were stupid, but because they were afraid to try something new. Ideas became culture through repetition, and ideas survive by repetition. Most people defended habits more than truth, and people trusted familiar stories. The easiest thought was the borrowed one, and thinking was harder than repeating. Tommy glanced at him, then back at the window, his hands clenched in his lap. Southwick felt the cold of that moment, not in his hands, but in the marrow of his bones. He had known this feeling before, when he had stayed in a room with no heat, and the noise filled the room like smoke, and the rumors bounced like echoes—things said and then forgotten, then repeated again, until they were just the sound of the world pretending to be real. A repeated lie eventually sounded ordinary, and Southwick thought of how the world had made him believe he didn’t deserve help, that asking was a sign of weakness. The moment to ask for help passed, and he knew it before he said it aloud. He opened his mouth, then closed it. The wind picked up, and the world outside seemed to shrink, as if the city had pulled back its edges.  

Southwick stood and walked to the driver’s side, his boots sinking slightly into the puddles. The wet cobblestones gave way under his weight, and the sound of his footsteps echoed through the night, a rhythm that felt both like a warning and a promise. He turned back, hesitated, then sat down again. The wind now carried the sound of the street musician, a soft, persistent hum, and the streetlamp flickered once more, then steadied. Tommy didn’t move, his eyes down, hands in pockets, silent as the street itself. Southwick looked back at the car, its lights dark, its engine cold, and for a moment, he thought of how the world had once been open, and how it had closed itself up around them. He didn’t speak. He didn’t need to. He turned and walked, slowly, toward the edge of the street, where the water met the shore. Tommy followed, silent, the wind whispering between them, carrying the weight of the moment they had failed to ask. The wet pavement reflected the streetlights again, and for a second, the city looked like it was remembering. Southwick stopped, looked back at the car, and did not speak. The wind carried the sound of the street musician, and the streetlamp flickered once, then steadied.

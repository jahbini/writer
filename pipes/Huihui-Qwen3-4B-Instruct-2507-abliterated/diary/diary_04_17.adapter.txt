Hi, Friend,  

It’s been a week since I’ve sat down and actually *looked* at the sky — not just at the way it’s been drizzling in patches, like someone’s trying to make it feel dramatic, but because the sky has started doing this strange thing: it’s remembering things. Like it’s holding its breath, waiting for a certain moment, and then, suddenly, the whole thing tilts.  

So, I was telling Southwick yesterday — this one, the one who’s been overseeing the estate — and he said, “You know, the executor’s been going through this whole thing, and it’s like the journey itself is the one who’s getting confused.” I said, “Oh, I didn’t know the journey had a mind of its own.” He said, “No, it’s just that the executor keeps speaking to the King of Poodepoo like he’s on a tightrope, and the King’s just nodding, like he’s measuring the rhythm of the wind.”  

Late morning, under that sun-dappled sky, the King of Poodepoo was walking beside the executor, and there was a cluster of white lilies at the edge of the path — fresh, just as if they’d been waiting. The executor pointed at them and said, “These have bloomed just as I passed them yesterday — same time, same breeze — so the path must be steady.” The King nodded, adjusting his sash, like he was checking the time on his watch. It was so quiet, so composed, and I thought, *This is how things should go — smooth, with no hiccups.* I swear, it felt like the lilies were breathing in time with the executor’s footsteps.  

Then, mid-afternoon, it started to drizzle, and the stone steps were sloping gently toward the west gate. The executor was stepping up onto a step, and all of a sudden — *bam!* — a memory surfaced. It was the nephew’s last letter, about the harvest — something the executor had promised to deliver, but hadn’t. He paused, hands clasped over his chest, and said, “I remember the nephew’s letter — about the harvest — yet I passed this step yesterday. How did I miss it?” The nephew, sipping tea, just looked at him, calm, as if he’d already written the whole scene in his mind. I thought, *Here we go — the rhythm is breaking.*  

By then, the executor had already started feeling like a bit of a ghost — like the journey wasn’t just happening, but *watching* him, and he wasn’t quite sure if he was leading it or just being led. He said to himself, *I should have asked the King’s nephew for a reassurance — perhaps even a map — before now.* But he just stood there, hands outstretched, as if the words would come, and then they wouldn’t. And the attacker — who had been standing just behind a row of trees, holding a folded scroll — didn’t speak. Just watched. Like the guard was trying to make a speech and the audience was made of silence.  

Then, as the light faded into golden, and the breeze stirred the leaves, the executor approached the archway where the lanterns hung, and there, behind the trees, the attacker was still there. The executor stopped, took a slow breath, and said, “I should have asked the King’s nephew for a reassurance — perhaps even a map — before now. But I thought I had it all under control.” And then, just like that, he paused again, as if the words were too heavy to carry. The King of Poodepoo, who had been walking beside him, now glanced over, and the attacker stepped forward, holding a map — but didn’t unfold it. Just stood there, as if the journey itself were in question.  

By dusk, the sky had turned hazy, and the wind was still, stirring the leaves of the avenue, and now three paths converged at the stone crossroads. The executor stood there, one hand on the railing, the other gesturing toward each path, like he was measuring distances. “I thought I’d reach the nephew’s home by now — by the third stage — but now I see three paths, each with a different landmark. Which one is correct?” The King of Poodepoo tilted his head, uncertain, and the attacker stepped forward, holding the map, but still didn’t unfold it. It was as if the journey wasn’t just being interrupted — it was being rewritten.  

And that’s when it hit me — the treasure. Sacred, of course, and meant to go to the nephew. The guard had it all under control, and then, all of a sudden, the paths split. The King of Poodepoo, who had been so steady, now looked like he was trying to remember which step he’d taken last. And the attacker — who had been quiet all along — just stood there, as if the whole thing was a performance, and the audience was the one who hadn’t seen the script.  

I asked Southwick if the attacker had a name, and he said, “No, just ‘the one who arrived late.’” I said, “Well, maybe that’s the point — the one who arrives late is the one who’s been waiting for the journey to begin.”  

And now, the guard is still at the crossroads, just past the lanterns, and the nephew — the beneficiary — is still at the gate, sipping tea, as if he’s been there all along, and the whole thing is just unfolding.  

I don’t know if the treasure’s been stolen — or if it’s just been forgotten — or if the guard is still trying to find the one thing that’s been missing: the sense of arrival.  

But I do know this — the King of Poodepoo, who started the whole thing with such confidence, now seems to be listening to the wind, and the attacker, who didn’t speak, is the one who’s making the most noise — just by being there.  

And I keep thinking — is it the journey that’s broken, or is it just that we’re used to things being steady, and when they don’t move, we start to wonder if we’ve been walking in circles?  

I wonder if the treasure will make it — or if it’ll just settle into the soil, like the lilies, and grow there, unnoticed.  

But the nephew — the beneficiary — is still there, waiting. And that’s the one thing that feels true.  

So, I’ll leave it at that. The sky’s still drizzling, and I’m sitting in the garden, near the willows, and I swear, the lilies are swaying in time with the wind.

Hi, Friend,  

It’s been a week since I’ve sat down to write, and I’m still trying to piece together what happened at the Veylara estate—like a sentence that keeps folding in on itself. You know, the way things do when you’re not the one in the middle of the storm, just the one who’s standing in the rain, listening to the wind talk to the trees, and wondering if anyone else heard it either.  

So, here’s the thing: the executor—yes, the one with the satchel, the one who used to carry the King’s letters to the post office—was walking with the King of Poodepoo this morning, both of them moving like two pages turning in a well-worn book. Sunlight dappled through the wheat fields, and the path was clear as a bell—stone arch, then white gate, all the way to the estate. The King waved to a farmer who nodded, as if the route were a known poem, and the executor just followed, eyes scanning ahead, adjusting his satchel, keeping it steady, like a man who’d forgotten how to blink. I didn’t see him look at the world much—just the path, and the King, and the rhythm of their steps.  

Then, by late afternoon, the sky started to shift. Hazy. Rain came in soft, like someone had decided to soften the edges of the day. As they turned that corner, the stone arch was gone—swallowed by a cluster of new stone lanterns, which hadn’t been there yesterday. The King of Poodepoo paused, pointed at them, said, “These weren’t here yesterday,” and the executor frowned, checked his map, which still had the arch clearly drawn, like it was a promise. The king’s nephew stood near a weeping willow, watching, quiet, as if the change were a quiet hum, not a disruption. I thought, *This is the kind of thing that happens when the plan gets a little more than it can handle.*  

By dusk, they’d come to the stone bridge, where the king’s nephew stood with a lantern, and the attacker—someone who had been standing just behind, hands clasped, eyes fixed—stepped forward and said, “I believe the estate is beyond the next bend, where the willows meet the river.” The executor paused, took a deep breath, raised his hand, said, “Could you confirm this is the correct path?” The nephew nodded slowly, but the attacker stepped in between, and the executor’s eyes widened, though he kept his voice steady. He didn’t move. He just stood there, like a man who’d forgotten how to speak in sentences, just the rhythm of his breath.  

Then, at dawn, the mist came in, silver and soft, and the bridge was half-submerged, stones slick with dew, the stream flowing steadily. The attacker stood at the edge, watching the water, while the executor stepped onto the bridge—only to feel the stones shift, the bridge creak, and suddenly, the attacker said, “This is not the path to the estate—it’s the path to the river’s edge.” The beneficiary, who had been standing at a distance, now gestured toward the willows, confirming the estate lay beyond, now just a suggestion in the mist. The executor stood at the center of the bridge, hands clasped, eyes fixed on the water, as if the journey had become a question of arrival rather than progress.  

I don’t know if the treasure ever made it. I don’t know if the guard ever saw the attacker. I only know that the king’s nephew was there, watching, and the attacker was there, and the lanterns were new, and the bridge was half-wet, and the map said the arch was there, but it wasn’t.  

Southwick told me once that when a river changes its course, the fish don’t always know it either. I wonder if the treasure is still somewhere beneath the bridge, or if it’s just the way the journey was meant to be—like a conversation that never quite ends, just keeps unfolding.  

And the King of Poodepoo? He’s still in the village, sorting through his letters, as if the journey were a draft, and the final page is still being written.  

I keep thinking about the spin doctors, you know—how they make the storm seem like a mist, and the wind just a whisper, and the real thunder is beneath, waiting to be heard.  

But here, at the edge of the bridge, the attacker just stood there, hands clasped, like someone who’d been waiting for a moment that didn’t come.  

And the beneficiary? He just watched, from the willow, as if the whole thing were a slow dance, and the dancers were just learning the steps.  

I’m not sure if it’s a failure of delivery, or if it’s just a different kind of delivery—one that doesn’t need to arrive, but only needs to be seen.  

Maybe that’s the sacred part.  

Yours,
